Methodology

How a game gets its score.

Every number on this site comes from the same formula, applied the same way to every game. No sponsored placements, no thumb on the scale. Here's exactly what goes into it.

The formula

A Scouted Score blends three things: what critics thought, what players think, and how much game you actually get.

Critic score60%
The professional review consensus, on a 0–100 scale. It carries the most weight because it reflects many independent judgments rather than one.
Player rating30%
The share of Steam user reviews that are positive. Critics play a game once on a deadline; players live with it. This is the counterweight.
Hours of content10%
Typical time to finish, from HowLongToBeat. Length isn't quality — a tight six-hour game can outscore a bloated sixty-hour one — so it's deliberately the smallest slice, and it stops counting past a cap. It's there to reward substance, not padding.
60 + 30 + 10 = 100

Scored vs. awaiting a score

Not every game can be scored the day it launches. Scouted tracks two states so a thin early sample never gets dressed up as a verdict.

Scored

A real number

The game has a critic consensus and enough player data to stand behind. This is what appears in the rankings, and it's the only number Scouted presents as final.

TBD

Not yet

Missing a critic score, too few player reviews, or not out yet. Scouted tracks a provisional read internally so a game isn't invisible — but it shows as TBD rather than a confident number it hasn't earned.

What the tiers mean

The color of a score tells you where a game lands at a glance.

Why the gem? A 90 isn't just "a bit better than an 88." It's the point where critical acclaim, player sentiment, and substance all line up at once — which almost never happens. The cut-gem treatment exists because that rarity should be visible from across the page.

What Scouted tracks but doesn't score

  • Early Access. An unfinished game can't be judged as a finished one. EA titles are tracked and shown, but their score waits for 1.0.
  • Ports and remasters. Listed so you know they're coming, but a 2026 port of a 2015 game isn't competing for 2026's rankings.
  • DLC and expansions. Tracked against the base game rather than ranked on their own.

You can filter any of these in or out on the Rankings page. By default they're hidden, so what you see is this year's actual new releases.

Where the data comes from

  • Critic scores — aggregated professional review data.
  • Player ratings — Steam user reviews, all-time.
  • Length — HowLongToBeat community submissions.
  • Release dates, platforms, prices, artwork — storefronts and games databases, checked by hand.

The release list itself is curated manually. Every entry is matched and reviewed by a person, which is why the catalog is deliberate rather than exhaustive.

The honest limitations

  • Steam-only player data means console-exclusive games lean harder on their critic score.
  • Player review percentages can be skewed by review-bombing over things unrelated to the game itself.
  • Length data is crowd-sourced and thin for new or niche releases.
  • A single number can't capture whether a game is for you. It's a starting point, not a verdict.

Scores are recalculated as new data lands, so a game's number can move — most often in the first few weeks after release.
